01 · Independent project

A low-cost joystick-controlled wheelchair attachment

I wanted to see whether I could add powered assistance to a manual wheelchair without building an entirely new chair. I made a prototype for about $50, excluding a battery I received for free.

Arduino C++ DC motors Laser cutting 3D printing
Read the engineering case study +

The problem

Powered mobility equipment can be expensive, and insurance does not always cover the specific equipment or components a person needs. I explored a lower-cost attachment that could add joystick control to a manual wheelchair. DREDF research ↗

My design

I used an Arduino, motor driver, DC motors, laser-cut wooden board, 3D-printed motor mounts and wheel connectors, wiring, and a battery. I designed, wired, programmed, and tested the prototype myself.

The hardest part

The smaller drive wheel sometimes spun without transferring enough force to the wheelchair wheel. I experimented with motor torque and repositioned the drive wheel to improve contact and friction.

What I learned

A system can work electrically but still fail mechanically. I had to consider torque, friction, geometry, and motor control together rather than treating them as separate problems. The prototype worked with limitations and was tested without a rider; safe operation with a person was not established.

Cost

Approximately $50, excluding the battery.

02 · Team engineering project

An interactive action figure with a motorized base

Our team designed a poseable 3D-printed toy with movable joints and a rotating, illuminated display base. I contributed to CAD modeling, report writing, and the motorized-base code.

Onshape CAD 3D printing Electronics Prototyping
Read the engineering case study +

The problem

Our class team set out to create an interactive toy for children approximately ages 3–9, emphasizing flexible movement, durability, straightforward use, and engagement.

Design and implementation

We modeled the figure and base in Onshape and fabricated the structural parts using 3D-printed PLA. The figure used movable ball joints, while the base contained a motor, LED, button, wiring, and two AAA batteries. Pressing the button activated the light and rotation.

Iteration and testing

Across three prototypes, we revised joint dimensions, leg connections, and body proportions after encountering printing errors, restricted movement, and stability problems. We performed physical assembly, joint movement, pull, and drop tests.

My contribution

My documented team responsibilities included Onshape CAD modeling and the written report. I also wrote code for the motorized base. The project was collaborative, so the final case study will distinguish my individual work from the team's collective work.

What I learned

CAD dimensions have to account for real manufacturing tolerances. A model that looks correct on screen may print poorly or fail during assembly. Iteration and communication helped us improve the final design.
